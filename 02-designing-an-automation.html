Designing an automation.

Last lesson you learned to see the world as triggers and actions. Now you design one for real. Good automation isn’t about the tool — it is about decomposing a fuzzy manual chore into a clear map: a trigger, ordered steps, decision points, and honest error paths. Get the map right and the building is easy. Get it wrong and no tool can save you.

01 / 04

Decompose the chore into a map

Before you touch any tool, you draw the workflow on paper. Decomposing means breaking a vague chore — “deal with the inbox” — into the concrete pieces a machine can follow: one trigger, a handful of ordered steps, and the decision points where the flow branches.

Most beginners skip this and jump straight into clicking around inside a tool. It always ends the same way: a tangled flow that half-works and nobody understands. The fix is boring and powerful — write the map first, in plain language. What starts it? What happens, and in what order? Where does it need to make a choice?

A good map is specific enough that a stranger could follow it. “When a new email arrives, pull out the key details, then — only if it looks urgent — flag it for me.” Build one below by tapping a trigger, a step, and a decision point, and watch the map assemble into a single readable flow.

The map is the hard part

Once the map is clear, choosing a tool and wiring it up is the easy 20%. The 80% that decides whether your automation actually works is the thinking you do here — naming every step and every branch before a single connection is made. A messy map becomes a messy automation; a clean map almost builds itself.

02 / 04

Connect the fields between steps

Here is the single most common reason a beginner’s automation breaks: the steps all run fine, but the data doesn’t flow between them. Each step hands off a set of fields, and the next step expects fields with particular names. If you don’t connect them by hand, the next step receives blanks.

Think of every step as a box with output plugs on one side and input sockets on the other. Step 1 might output a field it calls email_addr. Step 2 has a socket labelled to. They mean the same thing, but the automation has no idea that email_addr should plug into to — you have to wire it. Miss one wire and that field silently arrives empty.

This is why automations fail “for no reason”: the flow ran, but a step got fed nothing. Press the button below to map Step 1’s outputs onto the inputs Step 2 expects, and watch the connections form field by field.

Wire it, then test with real data

Field mapping is fiddly, and it’s exactly where careful beginners beat careless experts. After you wire every field, run the flow once with a real example and confirm each value landed where it should. A five-minute test here saves hours of “why is this blank?” later.

03 / 04

Slot the AI step in at the right spot

An AI step is powerful, but a little slower, pricier and less predictable than plain logic — so you place exactly one, exactly where ordinary automation gets stuck. Usually that is the moment a step has to understand or write language. Everywhere else, keep it boring.

The trick is spotting the stuck step. If a step needs to decide what some text means, that’s a classify job. If it needs a specific fact buried in messy text, that’s extract. If it needs to write something, that’s draft. If it needs to shrink a lot into a little, that’s summarize. Flip each card to see a stuck step and the AI job that unsticks it.

One smart step, well placed

You’re not building an “AI workflow” — you’re building a plain workflow with one smart step slotted in where language lives. That mindset keeps your automation reliable, your costs low, and the one place things might go wrong small enough for a human to watch.

04 / 04

Error paths and a human checkpoint

Every automation you design must answer two questions the happy-path version ignores: what happens when a step fails? and who approves the risky moves? Skip these and your flow works beautifully — right up until the day it quietly doesn’t.

First, error paths. Steps fail: an input goes missing, a service is down, the AI step returns nonsense. A good design plans for it — retry, skip, or stop and alert a human — instead of failing silently and leaving you to discover the damage later. Silent failure is the enemy; a loud, logged failure is a friend.

Second, the human-in-the-loop checkpoint, and the rule is reversible vs. irreversible. If a mistake is cheap to undo, let the flow run and review later. If it sends something to a real person, spends money, or can’t be undone, put a human approval step in front of it. Design for the bad day

Amateurs design for the day everything works. Professionals design for the day it doesn’t. Add the error path, add a checkpoint on anything irreversible, and your automation becomes something you can actually trust to run while you’re not watching.

What to carry forward

Designing an automation is mostly thinking, done before you touch a tool. Decompose the chore into a trigger, ordered steps, and decision points. Wire the fields between steps by hand — the mismatch is the number-one beginner bug. Slot in a single AI step exactly where language understanding is needed. And design for the bad day: give every risky step an error path, and put a human checkpoint in front of anything you can’t undo. Do that, and you have an automation you can trust.

  • Map first: trigger → ordered steps → decision points, in plain language
  • Connect every field between steps — unmapped fields arrive blank (the #1 bug)
  • Slot in one AI step where language lives: classify, extract, draft, summarize
  • Plan error paths — a loud, logged failure beats a silent one
  • Human checkpoint on anything irreversible; auto-run only what is reversible
