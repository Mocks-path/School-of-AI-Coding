Five weeks. One system you can build.

This is the last lesson of the course. Everything you learned across five weeks — the programming loop and human judgment, building an app with an interface and state, giving an agent a model and tools, wiring automations from triggers to actions, and connecting it all with APIs — was never five separate skills. Meet your capstone: an AI intake assistant

Picture one small, real project: an assistant that takes a messy request from a person, figures out what they actually need, does the work, and replies. Underneath that simple promise, one request travels a short loop — receive, understand, act, respond — and then comes round again for the next one. Here's the shift that makes everything click: a working system isn't a pile of features, it's a loop. The intake assistant receives an input, uses a model's judgment to understand it, takes an action with real tools, and returns a response — then waits for the next request and does it all again. That loop-with-judgment is the very first thing you learned in Week 1, and it never went away; every week since has just added a richer way to fill one of these stages. The loop, not the pile

Notice it's a loop, not a one-way line. After it responds, the assistant comes right back to "receive" for the next request, carrying anything it learned. That's why one small, well-built loop can handle a thousand requests without you: the shape stays the same, only the input changes. Everything else in this lesson is about filling those four stages well — and reviewing them honestly before you trust them.

02 / 04

How you actually build it: plan to ship

"Build a system" sounds huge until you see it as a short, repeatable path: plan what it must do, build the smallest version, test it honestly, ship it small, then document it so future-you (or anyone else) can pick it up. Press play and watch the build journey run, step by step.

The order matters more than the tools. Beginners jump straight to building and skip the two steps that make the difference: a clear plan up front so you're building the right thing, and honest documentation at the end so the thing survives contact with tomorrow. In between, build the smallest version that works, test it against the ways it can fail, and ship it to a tiny real audience before you scale. This is the same disciplined loop whether you're writing code, wiring an automation, or assembling an agent. Watch the journey run.

Small and shipped beats big and imagined

The discipline is to keep every loop tight: plan a little, build a little, test it, ship it, write it down — then go round again to add the next piece. A tiny thing that runs teaches you more in a day than a grand design teaches you in a month. That's the same "start small, keep a human in the loop" instinct from every week of this course, now aimed at your own finished project.

Capstone: five weeks, now five layers

Step back and look at the whole assistant at once. The five things you learned that felt separate are actually five layers of one system, stacked from the judgment at its core to the connections at its edge. Click each layer to see the exact job it does inside your intake assistant.

Here's the picture nobody draws for beginners. At the base is programming — the loop and the human judgment that decides what happens. Around it, apps give it an interface, logic, data and state to hold onto. Agents add a model with tools that can actually take action inside that loop. Automation lets it run on its own, triggered by events instead of clicks. And APIs are the connective tissue tying every layer to the outside world — the source it reads, the model it calls, the place it sends the answer. Remove any one layer and the assistant still can't stand. Click each layer, then see how they lock together.

Judgment at the core, connection at the edge

The single most important idea of the whole course: the layers only add up because you own the judgment at the centre and let AI carry the volume around it. Programming gives you the loop, apps give it a shape, agents give it hands, automation gives it a schedule, and APIs give it reach. None of them is the "smart" part on its own — you are. That arrangement, not any one tool, is what you actually learned to build.

04 / 04

Before you ship: the whole-system review

A demo that works once is not a system you can trust. Before your capstone meets real people, run it past five honest questions — about cost, security, reliability, privacy, and upkeep. This is the review that separates a toy from something you'd actually stand behind. Reveal each check.

Every layer you stacked added a little risk, and this is where you pay it down. Each AI call costs money, so you cap it before a runaway loop drains your budget. Your keys and inputs are a security surface, so you never hard-code a secret or trust raw input. The system will fail somewhere, so you make sure it fails gracefully. It handles people's data, so you respect their privacy and keep only what you need. And someone has to maintain it, so you leave it simple and documented. Reveal the five checks — and treat them as the price of admission for going live.

The one sentence to leave with

AI didn't replace the builder — it gave one careful person the reach of a whole team, from the first line of the loop to the last check before launch. You've learned to plan and build, to test and ship, to connect five layers into one system and then review it honestly before you trust it. The tools will keep changing. This shape — a system where you own the judgment and AI owns the volume, built small, shipped carefully, reviewed before it goes live — is what lasts. Go build yours.

What to carry forward — and the whole course in five lines

A real system is a loop with judgment at its core, not a pile of features. You build it the same disciplined way every time — plan, build small, test, ship, document — and the five weeks you thought were separate turn out to be five layers of one stack: programming, apps, agents, automation, and APIs. Before it meets real people, you run it past cost, security, reliability, privacy and upkeep. That's the whole course, and it's now in your hands.
