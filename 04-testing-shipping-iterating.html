Week 2 · Lesson 04 · AI App Development

Building it is half the job. Shipping it is the other half.

You have an app that runs on your machine. That's a real achievement — and it's not the finish line. The gap between "works when I click it" and "works for a stranger on the internet" is where most first apps quietly die. This lesson closes that gap: you'll test the whole flow the way a real user would, design the states an app must handle, ship it to a live URL anyone can open, and set up the loop that lets it get better after launch.

01 / 04

Test the flow, not just the happy path

When you build something, you naturally test it the one way you meant it to be used — type a task, click Add, see it appear. That's the happy path. But real users wander off it constantly, and every one of those detours is a sad path you also have to handle. The app that survives contact with real people is the one whose sad paths don't crash.

Think of your app as a set of routes a person can walk through it. There's one golden route where everything goes right — and dozens of little side-routes where something goes wrong: an empty field, a double-click, a lost connection, a weird character pasted in. Beginners test the golden route once, see it work, and ship. Then a real user takes a side-route and the whole thing falls over. The fix isn't more talent — it's the discipline to deliberately walk the sad paths too. Watch the map of routes build below.

How to walk your sad paths on purpose

You don't need a testing degree — you need a suspicious mind and a short checklist. For every feature, deliberately try: the empty case (click Add with nothing typed), the wrong input (paste something strange), the double action (click twice fast), and the interruption (what if it's slow or offline?). Ask AI to "list the ways a user could break this feature," walk each one, and fix what falls over. The happy path proves the app can work; the sad paths prove it won't embarrass you when it meets the public.

02 / 04

Design the four states every screen must have

A screen is never in just one condition. The same list screen looks completely different when it's empty, when it's loading, when something went wrong, and when it's full of happy results. These are the four states — empty, loading, error, success — and a screen that only designs the last one feels broken the moment reality gets messy.

Here's the beginner trap: you build the screen with three tasks already in it, it looks great, you ship. But the very first thing a new user sees is the empty state — and you never designed it, so they get a blank void and leave. Then the network hiccups and they get a frozen screen with no explanation — because you never designed the loading and error states either. Every state you skip is a moment where your app silently fails a real person. Toggle through the four states below to see what each one needs.

Ask for all four states, every time

This is a place where a single sentence saves you a dozen bugs. When you ask AI to build a screen, don't ask for "a task list" — ask for "a task list with an empty state, a loading state, an error state, and the normal filled state." AI will happily design all four if you name them, and quietly skip three of them if you don't. Then walk each state yourself: force it empty, watch it load, trigger an error, and fill it up. Designing states is not extra polish — it's the difference between an app that only works in the demo and one that works in the wild.

03 / 04

Deploy: from your machine to a real URL

Right now your app lives on your computer — nobody else can reach it. Deploying is the act of moving it from your machine to a hosted address on the internet, so anyone with the link can open it. It sounds like the scary technical part, but modern tools have turned it into a short, mostly-automatic pipeline. Understanding the stages is what makes it feel routine instead of magic.

Deploying isn't one button that mysteriously does everything — it's a small assembly line, and knowing the stations helps you spot where things go wrong. Your app leaves your local machine, gets packaged into a shippable bundle, is uploaded to a host that runs it, and finally appears at a live URL anyone can visit. AI and hosting tools handle most of the mechanics, but you're the one who checks the live version actually works. Step through the pipeline below.

The one habit that saves deploy day

The classic deploy heartbreak is "it worked on my machine but the live version is broken." The cause is almost always a difference between your computer and the host — a setting, a file, a piece of data that existed locally but never made it up the pipeline. The habit that prevents it: after every deploy, open the live URL yourself as if you were a stranger — ideally in a fresh browser or on your phone — and walk the happy path plus one sad path. Deploying isn't done when the tool says "success"; it's done when you've confirmed the public version works.

04 / 04

Iterate: release, observe, improve, repeat

Shipping isn't the end of building — it's the start of a new, better loop. Once real people use your app, they generate the most valuable thing you can get: honest signals about what actually works and what doesn't. The job now is to release, observe how it's really used, improve the weak spot, and release again. Version one is never the good version; it's the version that lets you find the good version.

The trap after launch is guessing. You imagine what users want, build it, and discover they never needed it. The discipline is to let the real usage tell you where to spend your effort — the same "read the signal, make the call" habit from Week 1, now pointed at a live app. Below are four signals a shipped app sends you, and the move each one demands. Your post-launch loop, in plain steps

Iterating can sound abstract, so here is the concrete loop a solo builder runs after shipping. Release — push a small, tested change to the live URL, never a giant untested pile. Observe — watch how real people actually use it: where they quit, what they repeat, what they ignore. Improve — pick the single highest-value signal from the cards above and fix or polish just that, with AI writing the change and you verifying it on the live version. Repeat — and commit a checkpoint each time so every release is safe to build on. Small releases, observed honestly, improved one signal at a time — that's how a rough version one becomes something people rely on.

The one-sentence version of iterating

Iterating an app is a loop: ship a small tested change, watch how real people use it, improve the one thing the usage is begging you to fix, and go around again — trusting the signal over your own guesses. AI makes every fix faster, but the discipline to verify before you trust, and to follow real usage instead of your assumptions, is the human skill that decides whether your app gets better or just gets bigger.

What to carry forward

You now have the full arc from "runs on my machine" to "works for the world." Test the whole flow — the happy path proves it can work, the sad paths prove it won't embarrass you. Design all four states — empty, loading, error, success — because reality is never just the success screen. Deploy through a simple pipeline from your machine to a live URL, and always confirm the public version yourself. Then run the release-observe-improve loop, trusting real usage over guesses. Across all of Week 2, the pattern held: AI supplies speed and volume, and you supply the testing, the judgment, and the discipline to ship something honest. That partnership is what turns an idea into a working app.

  • Test the flow, not just the happy path — deliberately walk the sad paths too
  • Design all four states: empty, loading, error, success — never just success
  • Deploy is a pipeline: local machine → packaged → hosted → live URL
  • Always open the live URL yourself; "success" from a tool isn't confirmation
  • Iterate in a loop: release small, observe real use, improve one signal, repeat
