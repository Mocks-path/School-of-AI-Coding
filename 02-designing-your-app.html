Week 2 · Lesson 02 · AI App Development

Design the app before you build it.

The instinct is to start building the moment you have an idea. Resist it. An hour spent designing on paper — the screens, the people who use them, the data underneath, and what to leave out — saves days of rebuilding. None of this needs code. It needs four plain-language habits: thinking in screens and flows, writing user stories, sketching a data model, and ruthlessly scoping your first version. Master these and the building part gets remarkably calm.

01 / 04

A user story is the new blueprint

Before you draw a single screen, name who the app is for, what they want to do, and why. That one sentence — a user story — is the smallest, clearest brief you can write. Vague brief, vague app. Specific story, buildable app.

A beginner says "I want to build a fitness app" and then stalls, because that could mean a thousand things. A user story forces the useful specifics into a single line: "As a [person], I want to [do something], so that [payoff]." Name the role (who), the action (what), and the outcome (why it matters). Suddenly you know exactly what one screen has to let someone do — and you can hand that same sentence to an AI assistant as a crisp instruction.

Why the scope note matters most

Beginners skip the fourth row and quietly design a monster. An app can do anything — that's exactly the trap. A user story with a scope note pins it down: one person, doing one thing, on ideally one screen, for version one. "As a busy parent I want to save a quick note so I never forget it — one screen, one job" is a thing you can actually build this week. Everything else is version two.

02 / 04

Think in screens and flows, not features

Users don't experience your feature list — they walk a path. Open the app, land somewhere, tap, arrive on the next screen, do a thing, get a result. Sketch that path — the screen flow — and the app designs itself. Skip it and you build screens with no doors between them.

An app is a small set of screens with clear routes between them, like rooms with doorways. The most common beginner mistake isn't a bad screen — it's a screen with no obvious way in or out. Mapping the flow answers the only question that matters: from here, where can the user go, and how do they get back? The flow makes the build easy

Once you've drawn the path, the whole build gets simpler — because you now know exactly how many screens exist and how they connect. Instead of "make me an app", you tell an AI assistant: "three screens — a home list, an add-item form, and a confirmation — with a back button from each." That's a buildable instruction. The flow is the map that turns a vague idea into a short, ordered checklist of screens. Vague ideas make sprawling, doorless apps.

03 / 04

The data model: what your app remembers

Every app that remembers anything needs a plan for what it stores and how the pieces relate. That plan is the data model, and sketching it is a plain-language exercise: name the things (entities), list what you know about each (fields), and draw who connects to whom (relationships).

Picture a simple to-do app for a household. The things it remembers are Users, Lists and Tasks — those are your entities. Each has fields: a User has a name and email; a Task has a title, a done-or-not flag and a due date. And they relate: one user owns many lists, one list holds many tasks. Rule of thumb — if you can describe your app’s "things" and how they connect in plain English, you already have a data model. Naming it clearly now saves painful rework later.

The good news: it's a sketch, not a science

You don't need database training to draft a data model. Name the nouns in your idea — "users have lists, lists have tasks" — and you're most of the way there. Describe those nouns and their connections to an AI assistant in plain words, and it can propose the fields and structure for you to check. Your job is the thinking: what does this app actually need to remember, and how do the pieces relate? Get that right on paper and the storage part becomes almost mechanical.

04 / 04

Scope it: must-have vs. nice-to-have

The single most valuable design skill is knowing what to leave out. Your first version — the MVP, the smallest thing that actually helps someone — should do one job well. Everything else is a "nice-to-have" for later. This is where dreams quietly become finished apps. Flip each card to sort a feature into its right pile.

Every idea arrives with a dozen exciting features attached, and every one of them adds weeks. A must-have is anything without which the app is pointless — remove it and the core promise breaks. A nice-to-have is everything else: real improvements that can wait until version two, after real people have used version one. The discipline of sorting features into these two piles — and building only the must-haves first — is what separates shipped apps from forever-unfinished ones. Sharing sounds essential and rarely is for a first version. It drags in accounts, permissions and multi-user headaches that can triple your build. Prove one person finds the app useful alone first. If they do, adding others is a worthy version-two project.

The one-question scope check

Before any feature earns a place in version one, ask the blunt question: "If I remove this, does the app still deliver its one core promise?" If yes, it's a nice-to-have — write it on the version-two list and move on. If no, it's a must-have and it ships first. This ten-second sort is the difference between an app you finish this week and one you tinker with forever. The AI can build any feature you name — but you decide which few actually make version one.

What to carry forward

You can now design an app before touching code. A user story — role, action, outcome — is your one-line brief. A screen flow maps the path users actually walk. A data model names what the app remembers and how the pieces relate. And ruthless MVP scoping decides what ships first and what waits. AI can draft any of these with you, but the thinking — who it’s for, what it does, what to leave out — is yours. Next lesson: turning this design into a working first app.
