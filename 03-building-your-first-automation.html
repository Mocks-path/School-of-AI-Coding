Week 4 · Lesson 03 · AI Automation

Building your first automation.

An automation is nothing more than a sentence you teach a computer to run for you: when this happens, do that. You already think this way. This lesson turns that instinct into a real, working flow — choosing how to build, understanding how two apps actually talk, adding one step at a time, and dropping in an AI step where a judgement call used to need a human.

01 / 04

Three ways to build — pick the smallest that fits

Before you build anything, it helps to know the categories of tool you can reach for. They form a spectrum from dead-simple to fully flexible. The names of specific products change every year; the categories don't. Learn the spectrum, and any tool you meet slots neatly into one of these three.

The temptation is to grab the most powerful builder and feel professional. Resist it. Power you don't use is just complexity waiting to break. Almost every useful first automation lives at the simple end — one thing happens, one or two things follow. Reach right only when a real task refuses to fit.

The rule

Pick the simplest category that solves the task in front of you today. Move one notch right only when you hit a genuine wall — a branch you can't express, a step no box provides. The tool should be as boring as the job allows.

The category, not the brand

Products come and go; the spectrum is durable. If you can name whether a task needs a straight line, a branching canvas, or a custom step, you can walk into any tool — this year's or next year's — and know within a minute whether it fits. That judgement outlasts every logo.

02 / 04

How two apps actually connect

Under the friendly boxes, every automation does the same three things: it authorizes (gets permission to act on your behalf), it triggers (notices that something happened), and it takes an action (asks another app to do something). Understanding this once removes almost all the mystery.

There are two ways an automation can notice an event. It can poll — check on a schedule, "anything new? anything new?" every few minutes — or it can receive a webhook, where the other app pushes a message the instant something happens. Polling is simpler; webhooks are faster. The action end is always the same shape: your flow calls the other app's API — its official "here's how to ask me to do things" doorway.

Authorize once, then it runs

The part beginners fear — "won't it need my password every time?" — is solved by that first authorize step. You grant permission once, the tool holds a secure token, and from then on it acts on your behalf without you present. That's the whole trick behind an automation that runs at 3am while you sleep.

03 / 04

Build it one step at a time

The single biggest beginner mistake is building all twenty steps, hitting "run", and watching it fail somewhere in the middle with no idea where. The fix is a loop so simple it feels too easy: add one step, run it with real sample data, check the output, then — only then — add the next.

Running with real sample data is the heart of it. Don't imagine what the previous step returns — run it and look. Nine times out of ten the surprise is small (a date in a weird format, a name split into two fields) and trivial to fix while the flow is still one step tall. The same surprise, discovered at step twenty, costs an afternoon.

Small and running beats big and broken

An automation that does one useful thing reliably is worth more than an ambitious one that half-works. Ship the small version, live with it for a few days, and let real use tell you what to add next. Every step you add on top of a proven base inherits that reliability instead of gambling with it.

04 / 04

Adding an AI step to the flow

Here's where this course's earlier lessons pay off. An AI step is just another action in the flow — but instead of a fixed rule, it uses judgement. The catch: for it to fit between two other steps, you must give it three things and demand one thing back. Input, instruction, and a required output format — so the next step can actually read the result.

The recipe never changes: hand the AI the input (data from the previous step), a clear instruction (the one job to do), and a required output format (usually structured fields, not prose). Then your flow parses that structured output and the next action uses it. Skip the format and you get a friendly paragraph no machine can read — and the flow jams.

The pattern that makes it reliable

You don't hope the AI guesses your format — you spell it out: "Reply with JSON only: {date, add_person, budget}. No prose." That one demand turns an unpredictable chat model into a dependable flow step. Input plus instruction plus a required format, every time. Master this one pattern and you can slot intelligence into any automation you build.

What to carry forward

An automation is one sentence — when this, do that — made real. Choose the simplest category on the spectrum that fits, because power you don't use only breaks. Remember what's under the boxes: authorize, trigger (poll or webhook), action (an API call). Build one proven step at a time on real sample data. And when a step needs judgement, add an AI step — input, instruction, and a required output format it must return. Next lesson: keeping automations reliable as they scale.
