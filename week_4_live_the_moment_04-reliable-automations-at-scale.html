When a step fails — order the escalation ladder

A step that can fail (and every step can) needs a plan for failure. The pros wire in a ladder: try the action, and if it fails, retry a few times, then alert a human, then fail safe so nothing is lost — all of it watched by monitoring. Click the blocks in the order a robust step should escalate. A wrong click nudges you.

↻

Retry

The first response to a failure. Most errors are temporary — an app blinked, the network hiccuped. Try again a few times, with a growing wait between tries.

+ Fixes transient glitches automatically − Retrying forever, or too fast, makes it worse
🔔

Alert a human

When retries don't help, tell someone. A real problem — a wrong password, a changed field — won't fix itself. A human needs to know, and quickly.

+ Turns a silent stall into a fixable ticket − Alerting on every hiccup trains people to ignore it
🛟

Fail safe

Whatever happens, lose nothing. Queue the item, mark it "needs attention", and never leave a job half-done. A safe failure beats a messy one every time.

+ No lost or half-processed data − Costs a little more thought up front
💡 Read the ladder as a sentence: "Try it, retry if it blips, alert a human if it truly won't go, fail safe so the data survives, and monitor the whole thing so you'd notice even total silence." Build exactly that below.

Your escalation ladder, top to bottom

💡 The rule that saves you: retry the cheap, temporary errors automatically — but only escalate to a human once retries are exhausted, and always preserve the data so a failure is recoverable, not destructive. Try → retry → alert → fail safe, with monitoring over all of it.

Ship it, or keep a human in the loop?

Automation is safe when a mistake is cheap and reversible — and dangerous when an action is public, irreversible, or moves money. Those actions still deserve a human checkpoint: the flow pauses and waits for a person to approve before it acts. Judge each action: safe to run unattended, or does it need a human checkpoint?
