What Is an AI Agent? — the difference between talking and doing

Four hands-on activities. Assemble the parts that turn a chatbot into an agent, put the agent's think-and-act loop in order, separate hype from the honest picture, then lock it in with a quiz on when an agent is the right tool — and when it's overkill. By the end you'll know exactly what "agent" means, and what it doesn't.

Progress
1

Assemble the parts — what actually makes an agent?

An agent isn't a new kind of AI. It's the same language model you already met, wrapped in three parts that let it do things instead of just talk. Study the three parts below, then drag each capability into the part that provides it. Guess freely — wrong pills carry a hover hint with the answer.

MODEL

The Model — its brain

The language model that understands your request, reasons about it, and decides the next step. On its own it can only think and write — it can't touch anything in the world.

  • Understands a messy request in plain words
  • Plans and chooses the next step
  • Explains its reasoning back to you
TOOLS

Tools — its hands

The functions you connect so the agent can act: search, send, save, calculate, run code. Without tools, a model is just a chatbot that can describe an action but never take it.

  • Searches the web and reads results
  • Sends a message or writes a file
  • Runs a calculation or a script
ENGINE

The Loop + Goal + Memory — its engine

What turns a single reply into a persistent worker: a goal to aim at, a loop that repeats until it's done, and memory of what already happened.

  • Holds a goal steady across many steps
  • Repeats: act, check the result, act again
  • Remembers what it already tried
💡 Why the parts matter: when someone calls a tool "an AI agent," ask which parts does it really have? A model with no tools is a chatbot. A model with tools but no loop does one step and stops. Only a model with tools and a goal-driven loop is a true agent. And even the best agents today still fail a meaningful share of multi-step tasks on public tests — closer to a capable intern than an autopilot.

Myth or reality? — sort the hype from the honest picture

Agents are genuinely useful, and they are genuinely oversold. Believing the myths is how beginners lose money and trust. Read the two mindsets, then drag each statement to where it belongs. Some are hype the demos want you to believe; others are the honest picture builders live with. Wrong pills hover-hint the truth.

The hype

What the demos promise

Slick videos make an agent look like a tireless employee that never errs and needs no supervision. It's an exciting story — and it quietly teaches you to trust the wrong things and skip the guardrails.

The honest picture

What builders actually see

Agents reason and act in a loop, which is powerful — and they also drift, get stuck, and make confident mistakes. They shine when you give them a clear goal, real limits, and a way to check the result.

Myth
Hype · believe it at your peril
Reality
The honest picture
💡 The most expensive myth: "agents are reliable out of the box." They are not. A useful agent is one you've tested, fenced with limits, and pointed at a task where mistakes are cheap to catch. Treat it like a fast new hire, not a finished machine.
