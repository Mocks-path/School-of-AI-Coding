Week 4 · Lesson 01 · AI Automation

Thinking in automations.

This week we stop doing chores by hand and start describing them once so they run themselves. An automation is nothing exotic — it is a plain sentence: when this happens, do that. Learn to see any routine task as a trigger, a few ordered actions, and the occasional “only if”, and you can build reliable little workflows without writing much code at all.

01 / 04

Every automation is “when X, do Y”

The fastest way to understand automation is to stop thinking in “apps” and start thinking in one sentence: when X happens, do Y. Every automation you will ever build — no matter the tool — is some version of that sentence, with a few optional twists.

The first half, “when X happens”, is the trigger: the single event that wakes the automation up. A form gets a new response. A file lands in a folder. The clock hits 9am. Until the trigger fires, nothing runs — which is exactly what you want. Automations are patient; they wait.

The second half, “do Y”, is the action — the actual work: send a message, save a row, create a file, move some data. You can chain several actions in a fixed order, and because the order never changes, the whole thing is predictable and testable. Sometimes you add a condition — an “only if” — so the flow can branch: if the amount is large, send it for a check; otherwise just file it. Click through the flow below to see each piece in order.

Read it as one sentence

Whenever a tool overwhelms you with buttons, shrink the task back to the sentence. “When a new response comes in (trigger), save it to my sheet and send myself a summary (actions) — but only flag it if it looks urgent (condition).” If you can say the sentence out loud, you can build the automation. If you can’t, you don’t yet understand the task well enough — and understanding the task is the real work of this week.

02 / 04

Automation vs. agent: a fixed path vs. a decider

Two words get mixed up constantly: automation and agent. They are cousins, but they behave very differently — and picking the wrong one is a common, expensive beginner mistake.

An automation follows a path you drew in advance. Same trigger, same steps, same order, every single time. It is a train on rails: fast, cheap, and utterly predictable — but it can only go where you laid the track. An agent, by contrast, is handed a goal and decides its own next step as it goes. It is more like a driver than a train: flexible enough to handle situations you didn’t foresee, but harder to predict, harder to test, and quicker to surprise you.

The rule of thumb is simple and it will save you a lot of grief: if you can draw the path, automate it. Only reach for a deciding agent when the path genuinely can’t be drawn in advance — when the right next step depends on what the last step turned up. Most everyday work is more fixed than it feels. Agent territory

Why default to the simpler one

A fixed automation fails in boring, visible ways you can fix. A deciding agent can fail in creative, surprising ways that are harder to catch. Beginners do best starting with automations, adding a single AI step where judgment is needed, and graduating to full agents only once they can clearly say why a fixed path won’t do.

03 / 04

Where AI fits: as one step inside the flow

Here is the reframe that makes AI far less intimidating: in a workflow, AI is just one step — a box in the flow like any other. It is the box you reach for when a step involves reading or writing language, because that is the part ordinary automation can’t do.

Plain automations are brilliant at moving data around, but they are helpless the moment a step needs to understand something written in human words. That is the gap an AI step fills. And it usually does one of four jobs: classify (sort text into buckets), extract (pull specific facts out of messy text), draft (write a first version of something), or summarize (shrink a lot into a little). Learn these four and you can spot exactly where an AI step belongs.

Notice what stays true from earlier lessons: the AI does the fuzzy language work, but the flow around it is still plain, predictable automation — and a human still checks anything that matters. Click each AI step to see what it does and where it slots in.

One step, not the whole machine

You almost never want AI to run an entire workflow. You want a mostly-boring automation with a single smart step dropped in at the exact spot where language understanding is needed. That keeps the flow cheap and testable, and it keeps the one place AI might get things wrong small enough for a human to watch.

04 / 04

Should you automate this?

Not everything should be automated. The skill isn’t automating more — it is knowing what is worth automating and what to leave in human hands. A few honest questions settle it.

Good automation candidates share a shape: they are repetitive (you do them again and again), rule-based (the steps are clear, not a judgment call), high-volume (they happen often enough to be worth the setup), and reversible (a mistake is cheap to undo). The more boxes a task ticks, the stronger the case for building a flow.

The opposite shape is a warning sign: tasks that are rare, high-stakes, or full of judgment usually cost more to automate badly than to simply do by hand. Move the sliders below to weigh a task on these dimensions and see where it lands — automate, pilot, or keep human.

Automate the chore, not the choice

The honest goal for a beginner isn’t a fully automated life — it is reclaiming the repetitive minutes so your attention goes to the decisions that actually need a person. Automate the chore. Keep the choice. Next lesson, we take one real workflow and design it end to end.

What to carry forward

You now have the mental model the rest of the week stands on. Every automation is a sentence — when X happens, do Y — with the occasional “only if” to branch it. Automations follow a fixed path; agents decide their own, so prefer the fixed path whenever you can draw it. AI enters as a single step that reads or writes language: classify, extract, draft, summarize. And before you build anything, ask whether the task is even worth automating.
