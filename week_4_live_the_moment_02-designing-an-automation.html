Week 4 · AI Automation

Designing an Automation — from a messy manual process to a flow you can trust

Four hands-on activities. Pick the steps where an AI step genuinely helps, plan the fallback for each way things can fail, map the data fields from one app into the next, then decide which actions can run on their own and which need a human to approve. This is how you design an automation on paper before you build it.

Progress
1

Where does an AI step actually belong?

Designing an automation starts by breaking a manual process into ordered steps — a trigger, the work in the middle, and the decisions along the way. Then, for each step, you ask: does this need judgment on messy input, or is it an exact, rule-bound task? AI steps shine on the messy ones (classify, extract, draft, route); everything exact should stay deterministic. Below are the steps of an incoming-request automation. Tick the ones that are a good fit for an AI step, then check your picks.

STEPS

Break it into steps

List the process as an ordered flow: what triggers it, each step in sequence, and the decision points where it branches. You can't design what you haven't decomposed.

+ Reveals every step that could fail or need AI − Skip this and you automate a blur
DATA

Follow the data

Every step passes data to the next. A name here becomes a contact there. If the fields don't line up between apps, data silently goes missing.

+ Clean hand-offs = a flow that works − Mismatched fields drop data quietly
FAIL

Plan for failure

Real automations break — an app is down, a field is blank, a step is unsure. A good design already knows what to do when each step fails.

+ Failures handled calmly, not silently − No fallback = broken flow you never notice
💡 The test for an AI step: is the input messy and varied (classify a message, extract fields, draft text, route by meaning)? That's AI-shaped. Is it exact and rule-bound (add numbers, look up an ID, copy a value, move money)? Keep it deterministic. Below, keep only the AI-shaped steps.
Select the steps where an AI step earns its place. Leave off the ones that are exact and rule-bound — those should stay plain, predictable steps.
💡 Rule of thumb: reach for an AI step only where a plain rule can't cope — unstructured text, fuzzy classification, first-draft writing. For anything with one exact right answer (a sum, a lookup, a copy), a deterministic step is faster, cheaper, and won't hallucinate. Fewer AI steps, placed well, beats AI everywhere.
2

Design for failure — which fallback fits each breakage?

The difference between a toy automation and a dependable one is the error path — what happens when a step fails. There's no single right answer; the fix depends on why it broke. A temporary glitch wants a retry. A judgment call or anything risky wants a human. A harmless gap wants a log-and-skip. Drag each failure to the fallback that handles it best. Wrong pills carry a hover hint with the answer.

🔁 Retry automatically
Temporary glitch that should clear on its own
🙋 Escalate to a human
Needs judgment or touches something risky
📝 Log it and skip
Harmless gap — record it and keep going
💡 The one rule that saves you: a failure should never pass silently. Retry the temporary stuff a few times, hand the risky or ambiguous stuff to a person, and log the harmless stuff so you can see it later. The worst design is the one that quietly does nothing and leaves you thinking it all worked.
3

Plumb the data — map each source field to its destination

Most automations are really data plumbing: they carry a value out of one app and drop it into a field in another. The step that quietly breaks flows is field mapping — lining up "this field here" with "that field there". Get it wrong and data lands in the wrong box or vanishes. Click a field from the intake form, then click the destination field it should flow into. A wrong pair shakes; a right pair locks green.

Source — the intake form field
Destination — where it should land
💡 Why mapping is where flows break: two apps rarely name things the same way, and types have to line up too — a date field can't accept free text, a single "Full name" may need splitting into first and last. Map every field deliberately and confirm nothing is left unmapped; an unmapped field is data silently thrown away.
4

Auto-run or ask first? — the approval-checkpoint call

The last design decision is trust: which actions can the automation just do, and which need a human to approve first? The dividing line is simple — can you easily undo it? Reversible, low-stakes actions can run on their own. Irreversible or high-stakes ones (deleting data, moving money, anything hard to claw back) deserve a human checkpoint. Judge each action: safe to auto-run, or needs approval first?

Quick reflect — make it stick

A sentence each is enough. Writing it in your own words is what moves it from "seen" to "known". (Nothing is saved or sent.)
