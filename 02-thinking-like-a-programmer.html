Week 1 · Lesson 02 · AI Programming

Think in steps, not in code.

The hard part of building with AI was never the code — the AI writes that. The hard part is thinking clearly enough to describe what you want. This lesson teaches the mindset: break a goal into literal steps, ask for it specifically instead of vaguely, recognise a handful of core ideas in plain English, and read the code that comes back. Master this and the AI becomes genuinely useful. Skip it and it just guesses.

01 / 04

Decompose the goal into literal steps

Beginners describe a goal. Programmers describe a sequence. The difference sounds small — it's the whole game, because a computer can only follow literal steps, and AI writes better code when you hand it those steps.

A computer has no common sense. Tell a friend "make me a cup of tea" and they fill in a hundred unspoken steps. Tell a computer the same thing and it has no idea what a cup, tea, or "make" mean unless you spell each move out in order. Thinking like a programmer means slowing down and asking: what are the exact, ordered, unambiguous steps? Here's "greet each new signup by name" broken into literal steps a machine could actually follow:

Step 1

Get the list

Take the list of people who signed up today. If the list is empty, stop — there's nobody to greet.

Step 2

Take one person

Look at the first person on the list. Remember their name and email.

Step 3

Build the message

Write "Hi <name>, welcome aboard!" — putting their actual name where <name> is.

Step 4

Send it

Send that message to their email address. If sending fails, note it for later.

Step 5

Next person

Move to the next person on the list and repeat from Step 2.

Step 6

Finish

When there's no one left, stop and report how many greetings were sent.

Score your decomposition, don't guess at it

A good breakdown isn't just "a list of steps." It's balanced across six qualities. Put those qualities on a radar and every plan becomes a shape: a strong decomposition is a big, balanced hexagon; a weak one is spiky — great on some axes, collapsed on the "handle the empty case" or "handle errors" leg. That's the classic beginner request, and the AI's guess will be equally shapeless. The greet-signups plan, by contrast, is big and balanced. The shape tells you the truth your enthusiasm won't.

02 / 04

Vague in, vague out — enter with a spec

The single biggest lever a beginner has over AI code quality is specificity. The same tool, given the same task, produces junk or gold depending only on how precisely you asked. Where you sit on the vague-to-specific spectrum matters more than which tool you use.

Here's the uncomfortable truth about a vague prompt: the AI still answers confidently. It doesn't say "I need more detail" — it fills the gaps with guesses, and its guesses are average, generic, and often not what you meant. "Make a login page" gets you a login page, just not your login page: wrong fields, no error messages, no idea where the data should go.

A specific spec is the opposite. Name the inputs, the rules, the edge cases, and what "done" looks like, and the AI has nothing left to guess — so its draft lands close on the first try and needs fewer loops to fix. The abstract point lands harder with a real example. Same goal — "let users log in" — asked two ways. Read what each prompt actually produces:

Vague prompt
"Make a login page."
What you get: a generic form with an email and password box, styled however the AI felt like. No rules on what a valid password is, no error messages, nowhere for the data to go, no "forgot password." It looks done and isn't. You now spend five loops discovering everything it guessed wrong.
Specific spec
"Make a login form with email + password. Email must contain '@'. Password min 8 chars. Show a red error under the field if either is invalid. On success, print 'Welcome back'. Don't submit if either field is empty."
What you get: a form that matches your rules on the first draft. The edge cases you named — empty fields, short passwords — are already handled. Fewer loops, and the ones left are your choices, not the AI's guesses.

How do you know your spec is specific enough?

You read it back and ask: could a stranger build the wrong thing from this and still technically be right? If yes, a gap remains — name it. Specific means inputs, rules, and edge cases are all stated. Over-specified is a real (milder) failure too: burying the AI in so much detail it loses the thread, or dictating how when you only care about what. Aim for the top of the curve, not the far edge. The checklist in Section 04 turns "specific enough" into something you can actually count.

03 / 04

Five ideas that unlock all code

You don't need to memorise a language to read what AI writes. Almost every program — in any language — is built from just five plain-English ideas. Learn to spot these and unfamiliar code stops looking like noise.

The code AI hands you will be full of punctuation you don't recognise. Ignore the punctuation at first and look for these five patterns instead. They're the vocabulary of thinking like a programmer — the same concepts whether the code is one line or one thousand. The snippets below are illustrative pseudo-code, kept deliberately readable.

Concept 1

Variables

A named box that holds a value so you can reuse it. "Remember this, call it name."

# a box called name
name = "Aisha"
greeting = "Hi " + name
Concept 2

Conditionals

A fork in the road: do one thing if something is true, otherwise do another.

if age >= 18:
  allow_entry()
else:
  show_sorry()
Concept 3

Loops

Repeat the same steps for each item, so you write them once, not a thousand times.

for person in signups:
  send_welcome(person)
Concept 4

Functions

A named mini-recipe you can reuse. Define the steps once, call the name whenever.

def send_welcome(p):
  msg = "Hi " + p.name
  email(p.address, msg)
Concept 5

Collections

A list or table holding many values together — like a spreadsheet column of names.

signups = ["Aisha",
  "Ben", "Chen"]
count = length(signups)
Putting it together

Read the shape

Most programs are just these five, nested. Spot the loop, find the condition inside it, and the logic reveals itself — no syntax memorised.

for p in signups:
  if p.new:
    send_welcome(p)

Reading code the AI writes: 50 lines → the 5 that matter

When the AI hands you a wall of code, don't read it top to bottom like a novel. Funnel it: skim the whole thing for the overall shape (what are the big blocks?), narrow to the functions (what are the named recipes?), then the loops and conditions (where are the decisions?), and finally focus on the one line that does the thing you actually care about. Reading is a filter, not a slog.

Be honest about the goal here: you're not trying to understand every character. You're trying to answer one question — does this do what I asked, and is there anything dangerous in it? If you can find the line that matters and follow the decisions around it, you can catch a confidently-wrong answer. That's the whole point of reading. Understanding grows loop by loop; you don't need all of it on day one.

Turn "specific enough" into a checklist

Specificity is a checklist, not a feeling. Before you send a prompt to the AI, run it through the same eight questions and let the count — not your optimism — tell you whether it's ready.

Each item below is binary on purpose. "Kind of mentions the inputs" is a no. "The AI can probably figure out the empty case" is a no. What the thresholds mean

0–3: too vague — send this and you'll spend the whole session fixing guesses. Add inputs and rules first. 4–5: workable but leaky; the AI will still guess the edge cases you skipped, so expect surprises. 6–7: a solid spec — send it, and the loops you run will be your refinements, not its mistakes. 8: excellent; just make sure you haven't crossed into over-specifying the "how." Whatever the score, the real judge is the running program — the checklist just decides whether your first draft starts close or starts lost.

What to carry forward

Thinking like a programmer is a describing discipline, not a coding one. Break the goal into literal ordered steps, ask specifically instead of vaguely, recognise the five ideas that build all code, and read what comes back as a filter, not a slog. The AI writes the syntax — you supply the clarity. That clarity is the skill that survives every tool change.
